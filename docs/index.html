Monte Carlo stock simulator

It promised 50%.
It hit 73%.

10,000 simulated months for the S&P 500, from two engines. Then an exam at 179 past dates: does the “50%” band really catch reality half the time? The textbook bell curve caught it 73% of the time. Real 21-day blocks caught 54%, within luck.

Two engines

Engine 1: the bell curve

Geometric Brownian motion, the model under Black–Scholes. Every day is a draw from one bell curve, calibrated on the last 5 years of SPY.

ST = S0 · exp( (μ − ½σ2)T + σ√T · Z ),  Z ~ N(0,1)
13.8%μ, a year
17.1%σ, a year
21trading days

10,000 paths. Median after a month: $777.71. Chance of a gain: 58.2%.

Two engines

Engine 2: real 21-day blocks

The block bootstrap assumes no shape at all. It cuts the last 5 years into real 21-day pieces and resamples them with replacement.

future = blocki,  i ~ Uniform{ 1 … 1,260 − 21 }

Crashes come back, and in clusters, because a block keeps its neighbours. Median after a month: $781.97. Chance of a gain: 65.7%.

Two engines

Same size of risk

Drop all 10,000 endpoints into a histogram. Both engines agree on how bad a bad month is.

6.9% · 7.2%95% VaR, one month
8.7% · 8.9%expected shortfall

A VaR backtest alone would call them equal. The difference is in the shape.

Why the shapes differ

Markets are not a bell curve

5,026 real SPY days since October 2006, measured in standard deviations. A normal distribution allows 0.3 days beyond ±4σ. There were 38. Worst day: −10.9%.

Excess kurtosis 14.1: most days are calmer than the bell curve says, and a few are far wilder. So GBM's middle bands come out too wide.

The exam

179 past months, one question

Once a month from October 2011 to August 2026: calibrate on the 1,260 days before, simulate, then mark where the real price landed inside the simulated distribution (its PIT, a number from 0 to 1).

An honest model spreads the 179 dots evenly, about 18 per column. GBM piles them in the middle. The bootstrap stays flat.

The exam

Graded: GBM fails, the bootstrap passes

Share of real outcomes inside the central band. With 179 tests, luck alone moves this by about ±7 points at 50% and ±4.4 at 90% (the grey zone).

GBM: 73% and 96%, both outside the noise. Bootstrap: 54% and 91%, inside it.

Where it breaks

The checks pass: simulated GBM matches its closed-form lognormal within sampling error, the exam scores about 90% on data that really is a bell curve, and an independent review found no look-ahead. These are the limits it does not hide.

It doesn't forecast direction

μ is the 5-year mean return, which is mostly noise. The width of the cone is the information.

Volatility is constant in each run

After a crash it isn't. Modelling that is GARCH, a separate Quantum project.

Survivorship bias

Only stocks that still trade can be examined.

Long horizons have little power

15 years of test dates hold only about 14 non-overlapping years, so the demo says so at 1 year instead of drawing conclusions.
